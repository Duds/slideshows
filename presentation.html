Part I

Background and Context

Why This Study?

A Changing Professional Landscape

Accounting has historically centred on financial transactions and economic performance. The global business landscape's transformation demands a broader perspective (Gray, 2010).

Pressures driving change

  • Global Financial Crisis (2008): exposed the limits of a narrow financial-metric focus
  • Climate change: tangible business implications now beyond dispute
  • Social inequality: income, access, and opportunity gaps under sharper scrutiny
  • Corporate misconduct: ethical breaches and privacy violations eroding public trust

Stakeholder response

  • Investors, customers, employees, and regulators now demand transparency and accountability
  • Expectations extend beyond financial success to social and environmental responsibility
  • Accounting professionals must adapt: ethics, sustainability reporting, and non-financial metrics

Adams (2017); Mio et al. (2020); Gray (2010); Dillard (2008)

Research Objectives and Questions

Examining the expanding role of accountants in contemporary business

Objectives

  • O1. Explore the ethical considerations underpinning the transformation of accounting practices
  • O2. Investigate the integration of sustainability reporting and CSR into the accounting profession
  • O3. Analyse the implications of incorporating non-financial metrics in organisational decision-making

Research questions

  • RQ1. How have ethical considerations shifted accounting practices beyond financial reporting?
  • RQ2. What are the key components of sustainability reporting and CSR, and how do they impact organisations?
  • RQ3. What are the challenges and benefits of integrating non-financial metrics into decision-making?

O'Dwyer and Unerman (2016)

Methodology

Qualitative, exploratory, multi-case study design

Research design

  • Qualitative, exploratory paradigm
  • Multi-case study approach
  • Organisations selected on commitment to sustainability reporting

Data collection

  • Semi-structured interviews: senior accounting executives, sustainability officers, academics
  • Document analysis: annual reports, sustainability reports, internal documents
  • Literature review

Data analysis

  • Thematic analysis
  • Open, axial, and selective coding
  • Cross-case comparative analysis to identify commonalities and best practices

Ethical safeguards: Informed consent obtained from all participants  ·  Anonymisation and organisational de-identification  ·  Secure electronic and physical data storage

Part II

Literature and Theoretical Framework

The Shifting Role of Accountants

From financial guardians to stewards of social and environmental responsibility

Traditional role

  • Meticulous record-keepers and financial gatekeepers (Dillard, 2008)
  • Accuracy of profit/loss statements, balance sheets, cash flows
  • Compliance with GAAP and established regulatory frameworks
  • Purely quantitative: monetary values, profit margins, equity positions
  • Primary audience: shareholders, creditors, regulatory authorities

Expanded role today

  • Sustainability reporting: ESG performance measurement and disclosure
  • Ethical stewardship and stakeholder advocacy
  • Measurement and reporting of non-financial intangibles
  • Integration of social and environmental impacts into decision-making
  • Primary audience: all stakeholders, employees, communities, society

Five key drivers: Globalisation and market complexity  ·  Rising stakeholder expectations  ·  Environmental sustainability imperatives  ·  Advances in data analytics  ·  Regulatory and standard-setting changes (IASB, GRI, SASB, TCFD)

Dillard (2008); Gray (2010); Adams (2017); Nobes (2018); Bebbington et al. (2017); Debreceny et al. (2018); IASB (2013); GRI (2016); IIRC (2013)

The Triple Bottom Line Framework

Elkington (1997): organisational success evaluated across three dimensions

People (Social)

Well-being of employees, fair labour practices, diversity and inclusion, community impact, human rights, and ethical supply chain management. Accountants measure and report on workforce and community metrics.

Planet (Environmental)

Greenhouse gas emissions, energy and water consumption, waste management, biodiversity, and sustainable sourcing. Environmental metrics are not only indicators of responsibility but drivers of cost efficiency and regulatory compliance.

Profit (Financial)

Financial performance remains central, but is balanced with social and environmental performance. Ethical accounting ensures financial metrics are achieved without compromising people or the planet (Savitz and Weber, 2006).

The TBL reframes the definition of organisational success. It is the conceptual anchor for sustainability reporting, CSR, and the integration of non-financial metrics into decision-making throughout this study.

Elkington (1997); UN (2015); Savitz and Weber (2006)

Part III

Findings

Ethical Considerations in Accounting

The integration of non-financial reporting has expanded the ethical terrain (Gibson-Sweet and Hamilton, 2017)

Transparency and disclosure

Balancing completeness of non-financial disclosure against information overload. How much to disclose, which metrics to prioritise, and how to accurately represent social and environmental performance.

Accuracy and reliability

Rigorous data collection to avoid misrepresentations that erode stakeholder trust. Ethical lapses have significant repercussions for the profession's credibility.

Fairness and objectivity

Avoiding bias in metric selection and reporting. All stakeholders receive equitable, consistent information for informed decision-making.

Integrity and professionalism

The same standards of integrity applied to financial data must apply to social and environmental data. Accountants act in the best interest of clients within professional codes of conduct.

Conflicts of interest

Reporting on an organisation's ESG performance while financially tied to it requires transparent disclosure and, where necessary, recusal from specific responsibilities.

Regulatory compliance

Navigating GRI, SASB, and TCFD standards. Ethical practitioners stay current with evolving requirements and ensure compliance.

Gibson-Sweet and Hamilton (2017); Eccles et al. (2019); Dillard (2008); Bebbington et al. (2017). AICPA and IFAC have revised ethical codes to encompass social and environmental dimensions (Gibson-Sweet and Hamilton, 2017).

Sustainability Reporting

Evolution, standards, and their role in non-financial disclosure

Historical development

  • 1970s–80s: Early environmental reporting in response to public concern
  • 1990s: Triple Bottom Line formalised; social, environmental, and financial performance linked
  • 1997: Global Reporting Initiative (GRI) founded; first standardised framework
  • 2013: IIRC releases the International <IR> Framework: integrated financial and non-financial reporting
  • 2014: EU Directive 2014/95/EU mandates non-financial disclosure for large companies
  • 2016+: SASB industry-specific standards; TCFD climate-related disclosures gain traction

Key frameworks

GRI
Comprehensive ESG guidelines across all industries; most widely adopted globally (GRI, 2021)

SASB
Industry-specific material topics; sector relevance over universal coverage (SASB, 2021)

TCFD
Climate-related financial disclosures; increasingly mandated by regulators

IIRC <IR>
Integrates financial and non-financial information into a value-creation narrative (IIRC, 2013)

Elkington (1997); GRI (2016); IIRC (2013); European Commission (2014); GRI (2021); SASB (2021)

Sustainability Reporting: Benefits and Challenges

The practice offers significant returns but confronts persistent implementation barriers

Benefits

  • Enhanced transparency: fosters stakeholder trust and organisational accountability
  • Improved decision-making: ESG data reveals risk exposure and innovation opportunities
  • Stakeholder engagement: drives alignment with investor, community, and employee interests
  • Competitive advantage: attracts ESG-focused investment; strengthens institutional reputation
  • Strategic alignment: connects sustainability performance to long-term value creation

Challenges

  • Data collection and validation: resource-intensive, particularly for complex global organisations
  • Standardisation and comparability: framework variations across sectors impede benchmarking
  • Greenwashing risk: misrepresentation of commitments undermines the credibility of the entire practice
  • Cultural resistance: embedding ESG in decision-making requires a significant organisational mindset shift

Bebbington et al. (2017); Moon et al. (2019); European Commission (2014); PRI (2020)

Corporate Social Responsibility

An organisation's holistic commitment to ethical conduct, stakeholder engagement, and sustainable practice

Core CSR dimensions

  • Ethical commitment: integrity, human rights, fair labour, ethical supply chains (Carroll, 1991)
  • Stakeholder engagement: ongoing dialogues beyond shareholders; input shapes responsible practice (Moon et al., 2019)
  • Sustainable practices: reducing emissions, conserving resources, sustainable sourcing
  • Reputation enhancement: attracts customers, investors, and talent; mitigates reputational risk (Porter and Kramer, 2006)
  • Stakeholder trust: social licence to operate; long-term relationships and community support (Matten and Moon, 2008)
  • Long-term sustainability: resilience favoured over short-term financial gain

Case studies

Unilever
Sustainable Living Plan: specific social and environmental targets with regular public progress reports (Unilever, 2021)

Danone
"One Planet. One Health": business strategy aligned to sustainability; transparent environmental and social impact reporting (Danone, 2021)

IKEA
People and Planet Positive strategy: commits to producing as much renewable energy as the company consumes (IKEA, 2021)

Carroll (1991); Moon et al. (2019); Elkington (1997); Porter and Kramer (2006); Matten and Moon (2008); Savitz and Weber (2006); Unilever (2021); Danone (2021); IKEA (2021)

Integration of Non-Financial Metrics

Financial indicators alone provide an incomplete picture of organisational performance and risk (Eccles et al., 2019)

Why non-financial metrics?

  • Holistic assessment: environmental impact, social responsibility, and employee well-being affect financial performance indirectly but significantly
  • Risk management: reveals reputational, ethical, and environmental risks not visible in financial data
  • Stakeholder expectations: investors, customers, employees, and regulators demand ESG transparency

Key metric categories

  • Environmental: carbon emissions, resource use, waste generation
  • Employee well-being: satisfaction, diversity and inclusion, workplace safety, professional development
  • Social responsibility: community impact, fair labour, ethical business conduct

Integration frameworks

Balanced Scorecard (Kaplan and Norton, 1996)
Links financial, customer, internal process, and learning metrics to strategic goals

ESG Integration
Investors embed Environmental, Social, and Governance factors into investment decisions, directly affecting company valuation

Scenario Analysis
Models potential climate, social, or ethical disruption impacts on financial performance

Integrated Reporting (IIRC, 2013)
Presents financial and non-financial data cohesively to communicate value creation

Eccles et al. (2019); Moon et al. (2019); Matten and Moon (2008); GRI (2021); SASB (2021); Kaplan and Norton (1996); IIRC (2013)

Key Findings

Five findings from thematic analysis and cross-case comparison

# Finding Significance
1 Accounting has evolved beyond record-keeping to encompass social and environmental responsibility Redefines the professional identity and scope of the accounting function
2 Ethical considerations are now integral, affecting transparency, accuracy, and reporting standards AICPA and IFAC codes actively revised to reflect this shift in professional obligations
3 Sustainability reporting provides a structured framework for ESG disclosure GRI, SASB, TCFD, and IIRC frameworks increasingly adopted across industries
4 CSR enhances reputation and stakeholder trust when properly integrated into accounting practice Demonstrated by Unilever, Danone, and IKEA case studies

Part IV

Discussion and Implications

Challenges and Future Directions

Implementation barriers coexist with emerging opportunities for the profession

Current challenges

  • Data accuracy and reliability: non-financial data collection is complex, especially for global organisations
  • Lack of global standardisation: framework fragmentation causes inconsistency and impedes cross-sector comparison
  • Cultural resistance: embedding ESG in decision-making requires a fundamental organisational mindset shift
  • Greenwashing: misrepresentation of commitments undermines credibility sector-wide
  • Stakeholder complexity: balancing diverse and sometimes conflicting stakeholder interests

Future trends and innovations

  • Impact investing: dual financial and social/environmental return mandates gain mainstream traction
  • AI and blockchain: enhance data collection, verification, and transparent non-financial reporting
  • Climate scenario analysis: modelling ESG risk at board and strategic planning level
  • Integrated reporting maturation: wider adoption of the IIRC <IR> Framework
  • Mandatory regulation: expanding disclosure requirements (EU CSRD; ISSB standards)

Akpan and Oluwagbade (2023, pp. 168–169); IIRC (2013); GRI (2021); SASB (2021)

The Accountant's Expanding Professional Role

From compliance officers to advocates for stakeholders and sustainability

Paradigm shift

  • From financial reporting only to sustainability reporting
  • From shareholder primacy to all-stakeholder accountability
  • From compliance to proactive ethical stewardship

Stakeholder advocacy

  • Engaging employees, customers, suppliers, communities, and investors
  • Facilitating dialogues and gathering input
  • Balancing financial and non-financial interests simultaneously

Professional implications

  • Broader skills: sustainability knowledge, ethical reasoning, stakeholder engagement
  • Revised standards: professional codes updated (AICPA, IFAC)
  • New curricula: accounting education incorporating ESG, ethics, and integrated reporting
"Accountants are now at the forefront of addressing social and environmental responsibility, advocating for stakeholders, and contributing to a more sustainable and ethical business landscape." Akpan and Oluwagbade (2023, p. 161)

Gibson-Sweet and Hamilton (2017); Moon et al. (2019); Eccles et al. (2019)

Recommendations

For organisations and accountants, and for policymakers

Organisations and accountants

  • Commit to transparency: accurately disclose both financial and non-financial metrics to build credibility and stakeholder trust
  • Invest in continuous education: stay current on emerging standards, regulations, and sustainability reporting best practices
  • Conduct thorough risk assessments: include climate and ESG scenario analysis to identify and mitigate social, environmental, and ethical risks
  • Collaborate: partner with industry peers and standard-setting bodies to advance harmonised reporting frameworks

Policymakers

  • Regulatory mandates: consider frameworks that require sustainability reporting, particularly for large organisations, building on the EU Directive 2014/95/EU model
  • Global standardisation: support collaborative efforts among GRI, SASB, TCFD, and IIRC to reduce framework fragmentation
  • Anti-greenwashing enforcement: develop mechanisms to verify ESG claims and penalise material misrepresentation
  • Research investment: fund longitudinal studies on the effectiveness of regulatory frameworks in promoting responsible accounting

Akpan and Oluwagbade (2023, pp. 169–170); O'Dwyer and Unerman (2016)

Part V

Conclusion

Conclusion and Contributions to Knowledge

A transformative shift in the profession's scope, ethics, and social contract

Contributions

  • Illuminates the expanding role of accountants beyond financial metrics toward social and environmental stewardship
  • Highlights the critical need for ethical frameworks in non-financial reporting, and how professional codes are adapting
  • Underscores sustainability reporting and CSR as essential, not optional, accounting practices for contemporary organisations
  • Demonstrates how non-financial metric integration enables holistic, sustainable organisational decision-making

Directions for future research

  • In-depth industry and regional variation studies
  • Longitudinal tracking of accounting practice evolution
  • Effectiveness of regulatory frameworks in promoting responsible accounting

Limitations

  • Exploratory design: breadth prioritised over depth in any single context
  • Knowledge cutoff: September 2021
  • Industry and regional variations not addressed
"As organisations navigate an evolving landscape, transparency and ethical conduct will continue to be paramount, driving sustainability and responsible business practices in the years to come." Akpan and Oluwagbade (2023, p. 170)
